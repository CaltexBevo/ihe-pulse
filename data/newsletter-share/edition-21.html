Use AI to Strengthen the Thinking You Want to Keep

Eric So had written half a sentence when he handed a colleague’s question to ChatGPT. He asked it to finish his response professionally, made a few edits and sent it. Then the MIT professor realized how little of his own thinking had gone into helping her.

He recounts that moment in an MIT News interview about his book, The Collision: What AI Does to Us. His concern is that a task can be finished while the thinking it once required quietly disappears.

So describes polished presentations whose presenters do not understand the material. Preparing the slides, he argues, was also preparation for the discussion. Automating the product can remove the work that made someone ready to use it.

That changes what successful AI adoption looks like. In a separate MIT Sloan interview, So urges managers to look beyond finished output and ask whether people can explain their reasoning and defend their decisions. Counting how often employees use AI tells them much less about whether judgment is developing.

The same concern reaches junior colleagues. Removing routine tasks could create room for more demanding work, So argues, if organizations reinvest in guidance and opportunities to build judgment. Otherwise, the work disappears along with a route through which people learned to do it.

His positive case for AI is more ambitious than getting existing tasks done faster. Used as a tutor, he says, it can help people tackle material that stretches them. The opportunity is to spend less time on routine work and more on thinking worth getting better at.

Case Study

See the Reasoning Behind AI Assisted Homework

The student had set up the calculation correctly, then given it the wrong label. An AI tutor caught the mix-up. The student corrected it and continued, eventually explaining which stock portfolio offered the most appealing balance of risk and return.

An instructor reading only the final answer would miss that exchange. In a University of Washington statistics course, it was part of the work available to grade.

Emanuela Furfaro and colleagues used LLteacher to bring AI conversations into homework. Instructors supplied reference solutions and set the kind of help the model should offer. Students could ask for an explanation or a forgotten formula, but still had to work through the problem. The complete conversation remained available for assessment.

The study followed 134 consenting students. More than 80 percent of survey respondents said the assignments helped their comprehension and interpretation of statistical results, a report of perceived learning rather than a measured achievement gain.

Some assignments went further: the AI deliberately introduced a flawed step or definition, and students had to find, explain and correct the error. Questioning the model was part of the assignment.

Practical Tips

Plan AI Literacy Around a Real Analytical Task

The patient data are fictional. The decisions about using AI are not. In a University of Glasgow teaching framework, an analysis of sensitive clinical information becomes the thread connecting an entire AI literacy course, from understanding a model to explaining its contribution in a public report.

Vinny Davies and colleagues built the framework around four areas: Understanding, Development, Reporting and Safety. A worksheet carries the same analytical problem through each. That continuity matters: choosing to let AI generate code also raises questions about checking the code, documenting the assistance and protecting the information supplied to the tool. These decisions belong to the same piece of work.

The fictional case solves another problem for the course designers. Participants can discuss it in an open forum without revealing confidential details from their own projects. Everyone has a shared problem to examine, while the choices remain recognizable to people who work with real data.

The authors refined the framework through workshop feedback and have used it in an online course and two-to-three-hour training sessions. Their paper also describes plans to bring it into undergraduate and postgraduate skills courses.

The framework gives AI literacy a destination beyond familiarity with a tool. By the reporting stage, the learner has to explain where AI entered the analysis and how the resulting work was checked. Producing an answer is one part of the task; being able to account for it completes the work.

Research

Give AI Grading a Standard You Can Explain

A student can mention the right legal rule and then explain why it does not apply. An AI grader that finds the phrase has located evidence, but it has not yet understood the answer. That small distinction is central to Sebastian Nagl’s proposal for making AI-assisted grading more accountable.

Legal answers are an especially revealing test because good reasoning can take more than one route. A defensible choice early in an argument may make a later step unnecessary. An early mistake may also carry through several later steps without becoming a new mistake each time. Counting expected phrases cannot resolve those relationships.

Nagl’s synthesis compares eight evaluation approaches and proposes a division of responsibility. The examiner sets the criteria and their weights. The model offers a judgment for each criterion, supported by quotations from the student’s answer. Software checks that the quotations actually occur and calculates the total. The examiner reads the complete response and decides the grade.

Each part answers a different question. Are those the student’s words? Do they support the interpretation? How much credit does that reasoning deserve? A quotation check can settle the first while leaving the other two open.

The proposed tests are just as revealing. Paraphrase a sound argument. Remove a decisive reason. Add irrelevant material. Introduce a valid alternative solution. Nagl calls for specifying the expected scoring response before running these checks. The architecture remains a proposal, but it makes an otherwise vague promise of explainability concrete: a grading system should be able to follow what changed in the reasoning, not merely notice that the wording changed.

Practical Tips

Follow an AI Citation All the Way to the Evidence

The current answer was on a page the AI could reach. Sometimes the model even cited that page, then gave the outdated answer anyway.

Owen Walters, Lihu Chen and Francesca Toni at Imperial College London followed this failure through search-assisted AI systems. They assembled 225 facts that had changed, including athletes moving to different teams. In their controlled tests, the researchers made sure the page containing the updated fact was always available. A failed answer could no longer be explained simply by missing information.

Search results offered one clue. Across the pooled controlled episodes, models relying only on snippets gave outdated answers 6.4 percent of the time. For episodes in which a model opened and cited a page, the figure was 1.3 percent. Opening the evidence was associated with fewer errors, although that comparison alone does not show what caused the improvement.

The team then changed the route to the evidence. For 50 claims about athletes who had changed teams, they supplied the source page directly. One model went from endorsing 14 outdated claims to endorsing none. Other models responded differently.

Their investigation also reached inside the models. In some failed responses, the updated information gained influence during processing before the old answer prevailed. The system had encountered useful evidence without carrying it through to the final claim.

That makes the citation itself an incomplete assurance. The linked page may be current and relevant while the answer beside it is wrong. Following the link all the way through means checking whether the page supports the particular claim, including the version of the fact that is true now.

Beyond Ed

AI Improved Forecasts Several Weeks Ahead

On November 20, 2025, a weather forecast picked up a cold outbreak headed for the eastern United States in mid-December. The leading conventionally corrected model used for comparison missed it. The successful forecast came from a system that had learned how existing forecasts tend to go wrong.

That is the idea behind research led by Hannah Guan and Soukayna Mouatadid and published in Nature. Their team added a machine-learning correction to forecasting models, using past predictions and the weather that actually followed to identify recurring errors. The system adjusts the probabilities those models assign to possible conditions.

It works on weekly outlooks, such as whether a week will be unusually cold or wet. Those are useful questions even when an exact forecast for a particular day remains out of reach.

The team’s MicroDuet system led every tested weather variable and forecast horizon in the September–November 2025 season of an international forecasting competition. The tests covered weekly temperature, precipitation and pressure roughly three and four weeks ahead.

For MIT researcher and co-author Judah Cohen, the reason to push that far ahead is preparation time. He told MIT News that energy officials had described the difference between two days’ warning of an ice storm and two weeks: the longer window could let them prepare adequately.

The resulting model does not need a supercomputer to run. Cohen says Kenya’s meteorological office has approached the team about using it for seasonal rains. Because the correction is an add-on, forecasting agencies could improve systems they already have, with more warning for decisions that cannot wait until a storm is on the doorstep.

Research

Know When a Rating Scale Is Shaping Your AI Research Results

Zero seems like a harmless number until it means different things to a researcher and an AI model. In a study of automated text scoring, the authors found that models often appeared to treat zero as the absence of toxicity, even when the rating scale extended below zero. After the researchers converted the scores to a common range, that interpretation could make texts appear more toxic.

Wonjeong Jo, Gyuwan Kim and Ming Shan Hee investigated how much the numbers offered to a model shape its judgments. Five models scored 11,700 texts for political leaning, movie-review sentiment and toxicity. The researchers tried 16 scale designs, varying the numerical range, the availability of negative numbers and whether the model could use fractional values.

If these were simply different units for the same judgment, converting every score to a zero-to-one range should have made the answers comparable. Instead, changing a scale feature shifted scores by roughly one-tenth of that range on average. About one-fifth of texts moved between low, middle and high categories.

More detailed rubric prompts did not remove the overall sensitivity. The model was interpreting the scale while deciding what the text meant; the later arithmetic could not necessarily reverse that decision.

The authors recommend checking alternative defensible scales, matching the endpoints to the concept and comparing results with suitable human judgments.

Practical Tips

Give an AI Replication Project a Clear Route Forward

For three chemistry students trying to reproduce a published AI model, repeated training failures led them to try a different computing environment. A fresh Linux cloud setup succeeded where their Windows setup had failed. Getting past that obstacle exposed the next one: the data did not adequately cover the enzymes and substrates they wanted to study.

The students and their tutor at Rotterdam University of Applied Sciences spent four months reproducing and extending ProSmith, an enzyme-related prediction model. They began without programming or data-science backgrounds and used AI coding assistants. Their account is useful because it follows the work beyond the moment an assistant produces code.

To improve the data, the team traced database labels back to original publications and assembled additional examples. They also moved from simulated negative examples to experimentally determined negatives, evidence about interactions that did not occur. That altered what the model could learn from, rather than simply adjusting its settings.

A later concern about data leakage turned out not to be leakage. Investigating it nevertheless led the team to preserve a training-only model, save models and weights automatically, and keep predictions connected to their original input records.

The researchers organized these experiences into PRAISE, a six-stage workflow spanning the question, data, computing setup, execution, validation and deployment. Each stage has an output, with routes back when a later problem requires an earlier decision to change.

By the end, the prediction script ran on the research cloud. The new enzyme predictions still needed experimental validation. The team had reached a meaningful milestone: a working computational process that could produce candidates for the laboratory to investigate.

Beyond Ed

A Neural Bypass Helped a Man Grasp Objects and Sense Touch

He could grasp his sister’s hand and feel the contact. In a Nature Medicine study involving one man with tetraplegia, a system linking brain signals, muscle stimulation and touch feedback made that exchange possible. The researchers also documented assisted drinking and self-feeding.

Making a hand close was only part of the challenge. Holding something fragile requires adjusting pressure as contact changes. The team tested that problem with hollow eggshells, where too much force would defeat the purpose of a successful grasp.

Two AI components did different jobs. A decoder interpreted brain activity associated with the participant’s intention to move. A separate controller, trained through reinforcement learning in a simulation, continuously adjusted grip force. Electrical stimulation activated muscles, while a powered hand support helped where stimulation alone was insufficient.

In the eggshell trials, force stayed below the prescribed threshold in an average of 87 percent of trials with the controller, compared with 27 percent without it. Those figures describe force regulation in that task, rather than overall hand-function recovery.

Touch completed another part of the connection. Sensors measured grip and triggered stimulation in the brain’s sensory cortex, providing feedback associated with the participant’s own hand. Intention could lead to movement, and contact could send information back.

Chandrasekaran, Wandelt and colleagues also reported rehabilitation changes beyond the assisted tasks. Right-wrist sensitivity remained improved for more than two months after combined brain, spinal and peripheral stimulation stopped. That lasting sensory change is distinct from the technology-assisted grasping. Together, the findings connect a demanding engineering problem to experiences with immediate human meaning: holding an object, feeding oneself and feeling another person’s hand.

Research

Make Lecture Descriptions Useful to the People Who Need Them

A lecturer points to a curve. The transcript captures the words, but the curve has no label. For someone who cannot see the gesture, a faithful transcript can still leave out the explanation’s most important piece.

This is one of the problems examined in AD4Edu, a University of Zurich project drawing on 66 lecture videos to study audio description. Stylianos Psychias and Yingqiang Gao combined video, nearby speech, slide text and available pauses to work out what needed describing and when the system should remain silent.

Then they asked a more revealing question: did descriptions that satisfied the evaluation rules actually give blind and low-vision listeners what they wanted?

Four raters each compared 400 pairs of descriptions. Rules concerned with pointing gestures aligned closely with their preferences, agreeing in nearly nine out of ten decided comparisons. A rule concerned with description length agreed only about one time in six. Listeners often preferred the fuller account, even when the automated criterion favored brevity.

The listening conditions help explain what that result means. Descriptions were played separately, so extra detail did not talk over the lecturer. The finding does not settle how much narration belongs in a busy recording. It does reveal a cost that a brevity score can miss: cutting words can also cut information the listener needs.

Source: AD4EDU: Benchmarking University Lecture Audio Description across Accessibility Standards, Metrics, and Learner Preferences →
