### DEEP DIVE: The OECD Report That Changes the Conversation (0:20–1:50)

The OECD Digital Education Outlook 2026 landed this month, and it's the most comprehensive international analysis of AI in education we've seen. Let me cut to what matters.

Researchers tracked over a thousand students. The group using general-purpose AI tools — think ChatGPT, regular chatbots — produced dramatically better work. Up to 127% improvement. Sounds like a win, right?

Here's the catch. When researchers removed AI access for exams, those same students scored 17% lower than the control group — the students who never had AI at all. The AI wasn't helping them learn. It was learning for them.

But — and this is the innovator's edge — a second group used AI tools specifically designed with pedagogical intent. Think step-by-step hints instead of direct answers. Scaffolded problem-solving instead of generated solutions. That group showed sustained learning gains even after AI was removed.

The distinction is critical: it's not whether your students use AI. It's whether the AI makes them think harder or think less. If you're choosing tools for spring courses right now, that one question should be your filter. Does this tool preserve the productive struggle of learning, or does it bypass it?

The OECD also found that about 37% of teachers across member countries now use AI for lesson planning. In some countries, that's over 60%. But they warn — and I think they're right — that if we outsource too much of the teaching design to AI, we risk eroding the professional judgment that makes great teaching great.

The institutions moving fast on AI curriculum aren't reacting to fear — they're positioning their graduates for the jobs that actually exist.

It's specifically designed for administrators and IT professionals who want to move from being, as they put it, "data-rich but insight-poor." If you missed session one on Tuesday, recordings are available, and the remaining sessions run through February 17th. I'll put the link in today's show notes.

And here's one that made me pause. An Honorlock survey found that college students are lukewarm on formal AI courses — not because they don't care about AI, but because they're already teaching themselves. They're learning on their own terms, through YouTube tutorials and hands-on experimentation, not waiting for their university to build a curriculum. It raises a question institutions need to sit with: if your students are outpacing you on AI literacy, what exactly is the value you're adding?
