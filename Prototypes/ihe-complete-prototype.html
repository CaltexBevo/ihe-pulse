ChatGPT

NotebookLM

NotebookLM

by Google

NotebookLM is Google's AI-powered research assistant that transforms how academics and educators work with source materials. Upload documents, and NotebookLM instantly creates summaries, discovers connections, and answers questions grounded in your sources.

What makes NotebookLM unique is its source-grounded approach. Unlike general AI chatbots that draw from broad training data, NotebookLM only uses the documents you provide, ensuring responses are directly relevant and verifiable. It even generates audio overviews — AI-hosted podcast-style discussions of your materials.

Faculty use NotebookLM to prepare for classes, synthesize research for publications, create study guides from textbooks, and help students engage with primary sources more effectively.

Key Features

  • ✓ Source-grounded responses (no hallucination)
  • ✓ Audio Overview (AI podcast generation)
  • ✓ Automatic study guides and FAQs
  • ✓ Citation tracking to source documents
  • ✓ Support for PDFs, Docs, web links, YouTube
  • ✓ Completely free to use

Best For

Literature Reviews Synthesize multiple papers and find connections

STAFF PICK

Gradescope

Gradescope

by Turnitin

Gradescope uses AI to dramatically reduce grading time while improving consistency and feedback quality. The platform automatically groups similar student answers, allowing you to grade by rubric item rather than by student — often cutting grading time by 50-70%.

Originally developed at UC Berkeley, Gradescope excels at STEM assignments but works across disciplines. It handles handwritten work, code, bubble sheets, and typed submissions. The AI learns from your grading patterns, suggesting rubric applications for similar answers.

Beyond efficiency, Gradescope provides detailed analytics on student performance, helping identify where students struggle and which rubric items need clearer explanation. Many institutions provide Gradescope through site licenses.

Key Features

  • ✓ AI-assisted answer grouping
  • ✓ Dynamic rubrics with regrade requests
  • ✓ Handwriting and code recognition
  • ✓ LMS integration (Canvas, Blackboard, etc.)
  • ✓ Detailed performance analytics
  • ✓ TA workflow management

AI Prompt Navigator

9 core techniques, 15+ ready-to-use templates, and practical guidance for effective AI collaboration in higher education.

🧠 9 Core Prompting Techniques

Click each technique to expand and view example prompts you can copy.

1. Zero-Shot Prompting

Use when: quick baseline answer without examples

▼

Definition: Ask the model to perform a task without providing examples. The model relies entirely on its training data.

Use Case (Higher Ed): Quick concept explanations, generating first drafts, brainstorming ideas.

When/Why: Start here for simple tasks. If output quality is poor, add examples (few-shot) or more context.

Explain the concept of social stratification in simple terms for an intro sociology class (~120 words) and include one concrete, everyday example.
List the three most important differences between photosynthesis and cellular respiration in a single paragraph for first-year students.

2. Few-Shot Prompting

Use when: output must match specific format, tone, or rubric

▼

Definition: Provide 2-3 examples of the desired input/output format before your actual request.

Use Case (Higher Ed): Grading consistency, generating feedback that matches your style, creating quiz questions in a specific format.

When/Why: Use when zero-shot doesn't match your expectations. Examples teach the model your preferred pattern.

You are grading short answers. Example student answer: "[paste example answer]" Example instructor feedback: "[paste your feedback example]" Now provide feedback on the next student answer in the same style: Student answer: "[paste new answer to grade]"

3. System & Role Prompts

Use when: consistent behavior, constraints, or persona

▼

Definition: Define the AI's role, behavior rules, and constraints upfront. Creates consistent persona across interactions.

Use Case (Higher Ed): Creating tutoring bots, maintaining consistent feedback tone, setting guardrails.

When/Why: Essential for student-facing tools or when you need the AI to maintain boundaries.

System: You are a supportive teaching assistant for first-year courses. Use clear, neutral language, avoid giving full solutions, and flag uncertainty. Role: Act as an encouraging writing tutor. Give concise feedback on the draft below tied to our rubric. Rubric: "[paste rubric]" Draft: "[paste student draft]"

4. Context Injection

Use when: outputs must follow your local readings, rubrics

▼

Definition: Paste relevant documents, rubrics, or course materials directly into the prompt as reference context.

Use Case (Higher Ed): Grading with your rubric, generating content aligned to specific readings, course-specific help.

When/Why: When generic AI knowledge isn't enough—you need it to work with YOUR materials.

Using the rubric below, score the student paragraph and give two specific suggestions tied to criteria. Course/Level: Intro Psych (non-majors) Rubric: "[paste your rubric here]" Student paragraph: "[paste student work]"

5. Step-Back Prompting

Use when: model should identify problem type before solving

▼

Definition: Ask the AI to first categorize or analyze the problem type, then solve it. This improves accuracy on complex tasks.

Use Case (Higher Ed): Complex problem-solving, multi-step assignments, diagnostic feedback.

When/Why: Prevents the AI from jumping to conclusions. Good for ambiguous or multi-faceted questions.

First, identify the type of problem this is and what approach would be most appropriate. Then, outline a 3-step plan to solve it. Finally, provide the solution following your plan. Problem: "[paste problem]"

6. Chain-of-Thought Prompting

Use when: reasoning steps are instructional or must be checked

▼

Definition: Instruct the AI to show its reasoning process step-by-step before providing a final answer.

Use Case (Higher Ed): Math problems, logic exercises, teaching students to think through problems.

When/Why: Makes reasoning visible and checkable. Helps identify where errors occur.

Solve the problem below and explain your reasoning in numbered steps (max 6 steps). Then give the final answer on a separate line labeled "Answer:". Problem: "[paste problem]"

7. Self-Consistency

Use when: single-run outputs vary, need reliable result

▼

Definition: Ask for multiple independent answers, then have the AI compare and select the best one.

Use Case (Higher Ed): Reducing hallucinations, getting more reliable answers on uncertain topics.

When/Why: When accuracy matters more than speed. Built-in fact-checking.

Produce three independent answers to the question below. Then summarize points of agreement, note any conflicts, and choose the best answer with a one-sentence justification. Question: "[paste question]"

8. Tree-of-Thought Prompting

Use when: multiple viable approaches, want options before deciding

▼

Definition: Ask the AI to explore multiple solution paths, evaluate each, then recommend the best one.

Use Case (Higher Ed): Lesson planning alternatives, assignment design options, strategic decisions.

When/Why: When there's no single right answer and you want to see options before committing.

Propose 3 distinct approaches to teach [TOPIC] to non-majors. For each approach, provide: - Learning objectives - One keystone activity - Pros and cons Then recommend one approach and explain why, given a 50-minute class and 25 students.

9. ReAct (Reason & Act)

Use when: task benefits from alternating reasoning with actions

▼

Definition: The AI alternates between reasoning about what to do and taking action, creating a more interactive problem-solving loop.

Use Case (Higher Ed): Research planning, complex project design, iterative problem-solving with student input.

When/Why: For complex tasks that benefit from back-and-forth dialogue and iterative refinement.

Follow a Reason → Action → Observation loop to build a research plan. Reason: Restate my topic and identify what information is missing. Action: Ask me up to 3 clarifying questions. Observation: Wait for my answers. Repeat once if needed. Then output a step-by-step research plan with milestones and potential risks. My topic: "[describe your research topic]"

DISCUSSION

10. Advising Email Draft

ADVISING

ADVISING

Student situation: - Current year: [YEAR] - Credits completed: [NUMBER] - Career interests: [INTERESTS] - Constraints: [PART-TIME/WORK/etc.] Provide: - Suggested course sequence - Key milestones by semester - Recommended electives for their interests - Questions to explore with their advisor - Resources for career exploration

12. Role-Play Script (Advisor)

TRAINING

Scenario: [DESCRIBE ADVISING SCENARIO] Include: - Student background/situation - Opening dialogue - Key decision points - Model advisor responses - Common pitfalls to avoid - Debrief questions for training Difficulty level: [BEGINNER/INTERMEDIATE/ADVANCED] Focus skill: [ACTIVE LISTENING/PROBLEM-SOLVING/etc.]

13. Syllabus AI Policy Draft

POLICY
Draft syllabus language for AI use in [COURSE NAME]. My approach: [RESTRICTIVE/OPEN/ASSIGNMENT-SPECIFIC] Policy should include: - Clear statement of what's permitted/prohibited - Rationale tied to learning objectives - Citation/disclosure requirements - Consequences for policy violations - Resources for ethical AI use Tone: Educational, not punitive Length: 150-250 words

14. Academic Integrity Discussion

ETHICS

Scenario type: [GRAY AREA/CLEAR VIOLATION/ETHICAL USE] Course context: [COURSE TYPE] Include: - Realistic student scenario - Multiple stakeholder perspectives - Discussion questions - Relevant policy considerations - No single "right" answer Designed for: [STUDENT DISCUSSION/FACULTY WORKSHOP]

15. Student AI Use Handout

POLICY
Create a student handout on responsible AI use for [COURSE NAME]. Include: - What AI tools are (brief, accessible explanation) - How AI can support learning (with examples) - Potential pitfalls and limitations - This course's specific policy - How to cite AI assistance - Tips for effective, ethical use - Where to get help Format: Friendly, visual-ready (use headers, bullets) Length: 1 page equivalent

⚠️ 6 Common Prompting Problems & Fixes

❌ Too Vague

"Help me with my lesson"

✓ Fix

Include course level, specific topic, learning objectives, and time constraints.

❌ No Audience Level

"Explain quantum computing"

✓ Fix

Specify your intended audience: "for first-year non-STEM majors" or "for graduate researchers."

❌ Missing Format/Length

Output too long or wrong structure

✓ Fix

State format explicitly: "in 3 bullet points" or "as a 150-word paragraph" or "formatted as a table."

❌ Overloaded Prompts

Asking for too much at once

✓ Fix

Break complex requests into smaller, sequential prompts. Build on previous outputs.

❌ No Role or Tone

Generic, inconsistent voice

✓ Fix

Assign a role: "Act as a patient writing tutor" or "You are an assessment specialist."

❌ No Iteration

Accepting first output as final

✓ Fix

Review, refine, and verify. Ask follow-up questions. Request revisions. Iterate!

FRAMEWORK

The Wonka-Lantern Framework: Creative & Ethical AI

June 17, 2025 • Dr. Norma Jones

Audio Version
12:30
🍎 Apple 🎵 Spotify 🎧 Audible 📦 Podbean

What if we approached AI integration with the creative wonder of Willy Wonka and the principled wisdom of Diogenes? The Wonka-Lantern Framework offers a new mental model for educators navigating AI adoption.

The Two Poles

The Wonka Mindset: Creative experimentation, playful exploration, "what if we tried..." thinking. This pole encourages educators to dream big, test wild ideas, and embrace the magical potential of AI tools.

The Lantern Principle: Ethical illumination, principled boundaries, shining light on consequences. Like Diogenes with his lantern searching for an honest person, this pole asks us to examine our practices critically.

Practical Application

When evaluating any AI integration, ask both questions:

  • Wonka: What creative possibilities does this unlock?
  • Lantern: What ethical considerations must we address?

Neither pole alone is sufficient. Pure creativity without ethics risks harm. Pure caution without creativity risks stagnation. The framework helps educators find the dynamic balance.

Framework in Practice

Try this exercise with your next AI tool adoption decision: Create two columns. In the Wonka column, list all the creative, exciting possibilities. In the Lantern column, list all the ethical questions and concerns. Then look for where the columns intersect — that's your sweet spot for responsible innovation.

TOOL TEST

ChatGPT Pro Deep Research: Worth It?

February 28, 2025 • Dr. Norma Jones

Audio Version
15:45
🍎 Apple 🎵 Spotify 🎧 Audible 📦 Podbean

OpenAI's ChatGPT Pro tier promises "Deep Research" capabilities for $200/month. As a higher education professional, is it worth the premium? I spent a month putting it through its paces.

What Deep Research Does

Deep Research is an autonomous research mode where ChatGPT browses the web, synthesizes sources, and produces comprehensive reports. It can spend 5-30 minutes working through complex research questions.

My Testing Methodology

I tested Deep Research on tasks relevant to higher ed professionals:

  • Literature review on AI in community colleges
  • Policy comparison across institutions
  • Grant landscape analysis
  • Curriculum alignment research

The Verdict

Strengths: Impressive synthesis, good source diversity, substantial time savings on exploratory research.

Limitations: Cannot access paywalled academic databases, occasional hallucinated citations, expensive for individual users.

Bottom Line: Worth it for administrators and researchers who frequently need broad landscape analysis. Less essential for classroom instructors. Consider institutional licensing to spread costs.
